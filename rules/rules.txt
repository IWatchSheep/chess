Step 1: When I select a chess piece, the program displays all its available legal moves.

Step 2: Identifying Pieces Without Legal Moves

If I select a chess piece that has no available legal moves, the program displays a message explaining that the selected piece cannot currently make a legal move.

However, chess pieces without available legal moves must still be considered when identifying forming chess strategies in Step 8.

A piece's inability to move legally does not automatically exclude it from contributing to a developing chess strategy.

Step 3: Moving and Confirming a Chess Piece's Move

If I select a chess piece that has available legal moves, I can choose a destination square by entering its two-character coordinate and pressing Enter.

Before the move is finalized, I am asked whether I want to confirm it.

If I enter "Y", the move is finalized, and the chess piece moves to the selected square.

If I enter "N", the move is canceled, and the chess piece remains on its original square.

The program then returns to Step 1, allowing me to select either the same chess piece or a different chess piece.

After I select a piece, the program checks its available legal moves again.

1. If the selected piece has legal moves, the program displays all available legal moves, and I can choose a destination square.
2. If the selected piece has no legal moves, the program follows Step 2 and displays a message explaining that the piece cannot currently make a legal move. I can then return to Step 1 to select another piece.

No changes are made to the chessboard until I confirm a legal move by entering "Y".

Step 4: I want all the pieces on the board to have their available legal moves listed before and after a piece's move to a square is finalized. These lists should be recorded and kept in two separate files: one for White's turn and one for Black's turn.

Step 5: Step 4 creates two separate files: one for White's turns and one for Black's turns. Each file should maintain a record of the previous four turns and the current turn for its respective player. The records should include all available legal moves before and after each move is finalized. No additional files are needed for Step 5 because the two files created in Step 4 will also serve as the history files for Step 5.

Step 6: Calculate and Record Three Full Turns Into the Future for White and Black

I want the chess program to examine three full turns into the future, which means three individual moves by White and three individual moves by Black, for a total search depth of six individual moves.

The program must examine every legal move available at each step, including all possible responses from the opposing player.

From the standard starting position, the number of possible legal move sequences increases as follows:

- Individual move 1 (White): 20 possible sequences.
- Individual move 2 (Black): 400 possible sequences.
- Individual move 3 (White): 8,902 possible sequences.
- Individual move 4 (Black): 197,281 possible sequences.
- Individual move 5 (White): 4,865,609 possible sequences.
- Individual move 6 (Black): 119,060,324 possible sequences.

The total number of search nodes across all six depths is:

20 + 400 + 8,902 + 197,281 + 4,865,609 + 119,060,324 = 124,132,536 search nodes.

These numbers apply to the standard starting position. As the game progresses, the number of legal moves and possible future sequences will change depending on the current board position.

Future-Search Files

I want two separate future-search files maintained: one for White and one for Black.

- The White file should record the three-full-turn future search from positions where White is the next player to move.
- The Black file should record the three-full-turn future search from positions where Black is the next player to move.

Each future search must account for both players' possible moves, not just the moves of the player associated with the file.

The two files should be updated as the game progresses so that their information reflects the current board position and its possible future moves.

The purpose of these files is to provide the future move information needed to identify and evaluate developing chess strategies.

The strategies are read on every position the search reaches, through all six individual moves. Counting a line out to six moves while reading the strategies only through four moves, or only through five, is not this search.

Search Depth for Lists 1 and 3

List 1 in Step 8 uses the current board position, the previous four turns, the current turn, and one full turn of future information to identify forming chess strategies.

List 3 in Step 12 uses the same future-search information from Step 6, but begins its strategy analysis one individual move into the future.

For List 3, the program must:

1. Examine every possible legal first move from the current board position.
2. Use the resulting board position as the starting point for evaluating future forming strategies.
3. Continue examining the legal move sequences already generated by Step 6.
4. Use the remaining five individual moves within the existing search to identify how strategies may form and develop.
5. Keep each strategy associated with the future move and board position that produced it.

List 3 must not restart a new six-individual-move search after advancing one move into the future.

The original search depth of six individual moves remains the limit for both List 1 and List 3.

List 3 therefore uses the existing search results from Step 6 rather than extending the search to seven or eight individual moves.

The same search results are also used to evaluate the potential captures associated with List 3 when creating List 4 in Step 13.

Performance and File Management

The program must preserve the complete three-full-turn search logic.

Performance optimization and file management should be handled separately without changing the required search depth or excluding legal move sequences.

Step 7: I want the history files and future-search files to be managed automatically so they remain organized, cleaned, and up to date as the game progresses. At the beginning of every new game, all four files should be cleared, and the information needed for the new game should be generated.

Clearing these four working files must never clear the permanent archive in Step 15. When a game is being recorded, Step 15 copies the four files before this step discards or overwrites anything in them. Step 15 does not change the way these four files are written.

Step 8: Creating List 1 — Forming Chess Strategies

The four lists begin with List 1, which identifies chess strategies that are currently forming.

To create List 1, the program uses the following information:

1. Step 4 and Step 5: The appropriate White or Black history file, containing the previous four turns and the current turn.
2. Step 6: The appropriate White or Black future-search file, examining one full turn into the future.
3. The 20 chess strategies: The program compares the information from the past four turns, the current turn, and one future turn against the 20 chess strategies to identify which strategies are beginning to form.

The program combines the information from these two files and evaluates it against the 20 chess strategies.

The results are stored in List 1, called the Forming Strategies List.

List 1 contains the chess strategies identified as forming based on the player's previous four turns, current turn, and one full turn into the future.

List 1 is made from those files. It is not made from a FEN, and it is not made from a different record invented by Step 15.

After List 1 has been made, a trained network may read the Step 15 archive and add a second assessment beside it. That assessment is labeled as the network's. It does not replace List 1, it does not change the scores 30, 45, 60, 75, and 100, and it does not change which strategies are listed.

Step 9: The list created in Step 8 (List 1) is sorted according to how fully each chess strategy has developed. The most fully developed strategy appears at the top of the list, while the least developed strategy appears at the bottom.

Step 10: Creating List 2 — Chess Strategy Captures

List 2 identifies the opposing chess pieces that can be captured by the strategies listed in List 1 from Step 9.

For each strategy, List 2 should record:

1. The name of the chess strategy.
2. The chess piece belonging to the player that can make the capture.
3. The opposing chess piece that can be captured.
4. The square occupied by the capturing piece and the square occupied by the opposing piece.
5. The potential cost of losing the capturing piece as a result of the capture.

Each strategy should be listed alongside its possible captures, clearly identifying which piece can capture which opposing piece.

The purpose of List 2 is to identify the potential captures associated with each forming chess strategy in List 1.

Step 11: Sorting List 2 — Chess Capture Values

The list created in Step 10 (List 2) is sorted according to the overall value of each potential chess capture.

The value of each capture is calculated by considering:

1. The value of the opposing chess piece that can be captured.
2. The value of the player's chess piece making the capture.
3. The potential cost of losing the capturing piece as a result of the capture.

Each capture is evaluated based on its potential gain compared to its potential loss.

List 2 is then sorted from the most valuable capture at the top to the least valuable capture at the bottom, taking into account the risk of losing the capturing piece.

Step 12: Creating List 3 — Future Forming Chess Strategies

Using the appropriate White or Black future-search file created in Step 6, the program examines the chessboard one individual move into the future.

At that future position, the program repeats the strategy-identification process described in Step 8, using the 20 chess strategies to identify which strategies are forming.

The results are stored in List 3 instead of List 1.

List 3 is then sorted using the same method described in Step 9, placing the most fully developed strategy at the top and the least developed strategy at the bottom.

The purpose of List 3 is to identify how chess strategies may develop after the next individual move, allowing the program to compare future forming strategies with those currently identified in List 1.

The program must repeat this evaluation for every possible legal next move, identifying the forming strategies associated with each resulting future board position. Each result must remain associated with the move and board position that produced it.

List 3 reads the Step 6 file. It does not start its own search, and it does not read a rearranged archive instead of that file.

After each candidate's List 3 has been made, a trained network may add a second assessment to that same candidate. The assessment stays tied to that move and that position. Results for different moves are never merged into one list. The original List 3 rows stay as Steps 12 and 14 produced them.

Step 13: Creating and Sorting List 4 — Future Chess Strategy Captures

List 4 repeats the capture-identification and sorting processes described in Steps 10 and 11, but uses the future forming chess strategies from List 3 instead of the current forming strategies from List 1.

For each strategy in List 3, List 4 should record:

1. The name of the future forming chess strategy.
2. The chess piece belonging to the player that can make the capture.
3. The opposing chess piece that can be captured.
4. The square occupied by the capturing piece and the square occupied by the opposing piece.
5. The potential cost of losing the capturing piece as a result of the capture.

Each potential capture is evaluated based on the value of the opposing piece that can be captured and the potential cost of losing the capturing piece.

List 4 is then sorted from the most valuable potential capture at the top to the least valuable potential capture at the bottom.

The purpose of Lists 2 and 4 is to identify and rank the potential captures associated with the chess strategies in Lists 1 and 3.

- List 2 evaluates and ranks potential captures associated with the current forming strategies in List 1.
- List 4 evaluates and ranks potential captures associated with the future forming strategies in List 3.

Step 14: Definitions for Chess Strategy Recognition, Development Scoring, and Capture Valuation

14.1 General Rules for Strategy Recognition

Each chess strategy is evaluated separately for White and Black.

The following rules apply:

1. Relative rank 0 represents the evaluated player's back rank, and relative rank 7 represents that player's promotion rank.
2. Strategy recognition uses geometric attacks. Therefore, a chess piece without an available legal move can still contribute to a forming strategy.
3. Capture lists consider only legal moves, and only when it is the evaluated player's turn.
4. The history window contains the evaluated player's previous four turns and the current turn when that player has just moved.
5. One full turn consists of two individual moves, also called two plies.
6. A strategy appears in the list only when its foundation, progress, or completion conditions are satisfied.
7. Future move analysis can increase a strategy's development score to 45 or 75 when the appropriate conditions occur within the next full turn.
8. A strategy completed only in a future move sequence remains classified as partially developed in the current evaluation.

14.2 Strategy Development Scoring

Lists 1 and 3 use the same development scoring system.

Strategies are sorted according to development stage, followed by numerical score and strategy name.

- Completion conditions satisfied now: Fully developed, score 100.
- Foundation and progress exist now, with completion possible within one full turn: Partially developed, score 75.
- Foundation and progress exist now, without completion during that turn: Partially developed, score 60.
- Foundation exists now, with progress occurring within one full turn: Beginning to form, score 45.
- Foundation only, or progress only: Beginning to form, score 30.

The sorting order is:

1. Fully developed strategies.
2. Partially developed strategies.
3. Strategies beginning to form.
4. Numerical development score, highest first.
5. Strategy name.

From the standard starting position, White's forming strategy list contains only Central Pawn Occupation and King Shelter, each with a development score of 45.

14.3 Chess Piece Values and Capture Calculations

The program uses the following piece values:

- Pawn: 100
- Knight: 320
- Bishop: 330
- Rook: 500
- Queen: 900

The king does not have a capture value because kings cannot legally be captured.

A comparison value of 10,000 is used only when ranking the king within the Fork and Skewer strategy rules.

Capture Calculation

Expected Gain: The combined value of opposing pieces captured by the evaluated player during the exchange.

Potential Loss: The combined value of the evaluated player's pieces captured by the opponent during the exchange.

Net Capture Value = Expected Gain − Potential Loss

Checkmate adds 100,000 points to expected gain when the original player delivers checkmate, or to potential loss when the opponent's recapture delivers checkmate.

Lists 2 and 4 use the same capture valuation system.

Captures are sorted by:

1. Highest net capture value.
2. Highest expected gain.
3. Lowest potential loss.
4. Strategy name.
5. Standard Algebraic Notation (SAN).
6. Victim square.

The same legal move may appear more than once when multiple recognized strategies are associated with that capture.

Capture Evaluation Rules

1. The initially evaluated capture is always played in the calculation.
2. Each subsequent recapture may be declined.
3. Only the cheapest legal recapturing piece is considered.
4. A pawn that promotes still counts as a pawn when determining the cheapest recapturing piece.
5. An exchange stops when continuing would produce an equal net value.
6. Promotion adds the value of the promoted piece minus 100.
7. An en passant capture assigns the captured pawn a value of 100. Subsequent recaptures target the capturing pawn's landing square.
8. A capture belongs to a recognized strategy when the capturing piece participates in that strategy and the captured piece occupies one of its target squares.
9. Kings are never treated as capture targets.

Capture Valuation Examples

- Queen captures an undefended pawn: gain 100, loss 0, net +100.
- Queen captures a pawn, then a knight captures the queen: gain 100, loss 900, net −800.
- Pawn captures a queen; the recapture would lose a rook: gain 900, loss 0, net +900.
- Knight captures a queen, pawn recaptures, and our pawn captures that pawn: gain 1,000, loss 320, net +680.
- Queen captures a rook, with a pawn as the cheaper recapturer: gain 500, loss 900, net −400.
- En passant followed by a rook recapture: gain 100, loss 100, net 0.
- Queen captures and delivers checkmate: gain 100,100, loss 0, net +100,100.
- Pawn promotes to a queen and the queen is captured: gain 800, loss 900, net −100.

14.4 The 20 Chess Strategies

Each strategy has conditions used to recognize its foundation, progress, and completion.

These conditions determine its development score and its position in Lists 1 and 3.

Strategy 1: Central Pawn Occupation

- Foundation: A friendly pawn occupies the d-file or e-file.
- Progress: A friendly pawn occupies d4 or e4 for White, or d5 or e5 for Black.
- Completion: Both central squares are occupied by friendly pawns, and both pawns are defended.

Strategy 2: Central Pawn Break

- Foundation: The opponent has a central pawn, and a friendly pawn remains on a file from c through f.
- Progress: A friendly pawn attacks an opposing central pawn.
- Completion: The turn window contains a pawn capturing another pawn on the c-, d-, e-, or f-file.

Strategy 3: Kingside Pawn Storm

- Foundation: At least two friendly pawns remain on the f-, g-, or h-file, at least one has moved, and the friendly king is outside those files.
- Progress: A participating pawn reaches relative rank 2.
- Completion: Two participating pawns reach relative rank 3 or higher, and the friendly king occupies a file from a through d.

Strategy 4: Queenside Minority Attack

- Foundation: The evaluated player has fewer pawns than the opponent on the a-, b-, and c-files, while retaining at least one pawn on those files.
- Progress: A friendly queenside pawn reaches relative rank 2.
- Completion: A participating pawn reaches relative rank 3, and an opposing pawn on the a- through d-files is isolated or doubled.

Strategy 5: King Shelter

- Foundation: The king is on e1 or e8 with a remaining castling right, or is already on c1, g1, c8, or g8.
- Progress: The king has castled, or no more than one piece remains between the king and a castling rook.
- Completion: The king occupies a castled square, is not attacked, and its three shelter pawns remain unmoved.

Strategy 6: Opposite-Side Castling Attack

- Foundation: The friendly king occupies the home rank on the a–c or f–h files, while the opposing king occupies the opposite wing.
- Progress: A friendly pawn on the opposing king's wing reaches relative rank 2.
- Completion: That pawn reaches relative rank 3, and a friendly rook or queen occupies a half-open file on that wing.

Strategy 7: Fianchetto

- Foundation: The b-pawn or g-pawn has advanced one square, or a friendly bishop already occupies b2, g2, b7, or g7.
- Progress: A friendly bishop occupies one of those fianchetto squares.
- Completion: The bishop attacks at least one of the central squares d4, d5, e4, or e5.

Strategy 8: Open-File Control

- Foundation: At least one file contains no friendly pawn, and a friendly rook or queen remains on the board.
- Progress: A friendly rook or queen occupies a file without a friendly pawn.
- Completion: A fully open file contains more friendly major pieces than opposing major pieces.

Strategy 9: Seventh-Rank Invasion

- Foundation: A friendly rook or queen exists, and at least one file contains no friendly pawn.
- Progress: A friendly major piece reaches relative rank 5.
- Completion: A friendly rook or queen reaches the opponent's second rank and attacks an opposing piece or pawn.

Strategy 10: Passed-Pawn Advance

- Foundation: A friendly pawn has no opposing pawn ahead of it on its own file or either adjacent file.
- Progress: The passed pawn reaches relative rank 3.
- Completion: The passed pawn reaches relative rank 5 or higher and is defended.

Strategy 11: Isolated-Pawn Attack

- Foundation: An opposing pawn has no friendly neighboring pawn on an adjacent file.
- Progress: A friendly piece attacks that opposing pawn, or a friendly rook or queen shares its file.
- Completion: The number of attacks against the isolated pawn exceeds the number of defenses.

Strategy 12: Backward-Pawn Pressure

- Foundation: An opposing pawn's advance square is attacked by a friendly pawn, is not defended by an opposing pawn, and lies on a file without a friendly pawn.
- Progress: A friendly rook or queen occupies the opposing pawn's file.
- Completion: That major piece remains on the file, and the opposing pawn's advance square is occupied or attacked.

Strategy 13: Knight Outpost

- Foundation: A square on relative ranks 3 through 6 is empty or contains a friendly knight, is defended by a friendly pawn, and cannot still be attacked by an opposing pawn.
- Progress: A friendly knight occupies or attacks that square.
- Completion: A friendly knight occupies the outpost square.

Strategy 14: Bishop Pair

- Foundation: The evaluated player has two bishops, while the opponent has fewer than two.
- Progress: At least one friendly bishop has moved away from the back rank.
- Completion: At least one file from c through f contains no pawns, and a friendly bishop has three squares of unobstructed travel.

Strategy 15: Pin

- Foundation: A friendly bishop, rook, or queen aligns with an opposing front piece and a more valuable rear piece or king.
- Progress: The attacking line to the opposing front piece is unobstructed.
- Completion: The rear piece is the opposing king, or a second friendly piece also attacks the front piece.

Strategy 16: Discovered Attack

- Foundation: Exactly one friendly piece stands between a friendly sliding piece and an opposing piece other than a pawn.
- Progress: The intervening friendly piece has a legal move that takes it off the attacking line.
- Completion: That move captures a piece or delivers check, while the sliding piece continues attacking the original target.

Strategy 17: Fork

- Foundation: A piece's current square or a possible legal move creates attacks against two opposing pieces other than pawns.
- Progress: Two opposing target pieces are currently attacked.
- Completion: Both targets satisfy the strategy's value conditions.

For a fork created by a sliding piece, its targets qualify only when they are kings, undefended, or at least as valuable as the attacking piece.

Strategy 18: Skewer

- Foundation: A friendly sliding piece aligns with two opposing pieces, and the nearer opposing piece is at least as valuable as the farther piece.
- Progress: The line to the front opposing piece is unobstructed, and the front piece is the king or is strictly more valuable than the rear piece.
- Completion: The front piece is the king, or it is more valuable than the attacking sliding piece while the rear piece is undefended or worth at least as much as the attacker.

Strategy 19: Major-Piece Battery

- Foundation: Two friendly rooks or queens share a rank or file, no more than two pieces stand between them, and at least one participating major piece has moved from its original square.
- Progress: No more than one piece remains between the participating major pieces.
- Completion: The line between the participating pieces is clear, and they share a half-open file or one attacks an opposing piece along that line.

Strategy 20: Promotion

- Foundation: A friendly pawn reaches relative rank 4 or higher.
- Progress: The pawn reaches relative rank 5 or higher.
- Completion: The pawn has an available legal promotion move.

A pawn blocked on the seventh rank remains at the progress stage until a legal promotion move becomes available.

14.5 Applying These Definitions to the Four Lists

The same strategy recognition and capture valuation definitions must be used consistently throughout Lists 1–4.

List 1 — Current Forming Strategies

Identifies and scores the strategies forming from the current board position, using the relevant move history and one full turn of future analysis.

List 2 — Current Strategy Captures

Identifies legal captures associated with the strategies in List 1 and ranks them using the capture valuation rules.

List 3 — Future Forming Strategies

Repeats the strategy recognition process one individual move into the future.

Each strategy remains associated with the legal move and resulting future board position that produced it.

List 4 — Future Strategy Captures

Uses the strategies identified in List 3 and evaluates their associated legal captures after one opposing reply, when the originally evaluated player is to move again.

The four lists use the same recognition and evaluation definitions to make current and future strategies comparable.

14.6 Relationship to the Existing Search and File Requirements

Steps 4 through 7 make and maintain the four working files. Steps 8 through 13 make Lists 1 through 4 from those files. Step 14 supplies the definitions those lists use.

Step 15 keeps copies of the files Steps 4 through 6 have already written. It does not replace those files, and it does not give them a new format.

The complete search remains three full turns, six individual moves. List 3 reuses that search. It does not extend it, and it does not shorten it.

============================================================
STEP 15: KEEP THE FILES MADE BY STEPS 4, 5, AND 6
============================================================

15.1 Why the archive exists

The four working files keep only four past turns, the current turn, and the future search. Everything older is deleted. Saved only at the end of a long game, they would hold the ending and would not hold the opening or the middlegame. Lists 1 and 3 could not learn from those parts of the game.

Step 15 keeps every recorded move of both sides, for the whole game, in one archive file. Each move is kept as three linked parts, taken from the files Steps 4, 5, and 6 have already written:

- the four past turns
- the current turn
- the future turn

All the pieces show in each part, because the history files already list every piece of both colors and its legal moves before and after the move. The future turn is the finished Step 6 file, not a four-ply or five-ply reading.

The next move's past includes the turn that was just the present, because Step 5 advances the window. Past links to present links to future across the whole game.

A FEN is not used in place of these files. Rebuilding the legal-move listings or the six-ply evidence from a FEN was tried, and it did not reproduce the files. Central-pawn-break is completed from the turn window in the history file. The six-ply evidence is in the future-search file. Those files are the records.

Step 15 does not change the chess rules, the 20 strategies, the scores 30, 45, 60, 75, and 100, or the six-ply search. It does not change how Steps 4, 5, and 6 write the four working files.

15.2 The four working files stay as Steps 4 through 7 made them

Recording a game does not change how the four working files are written, updated, or cleared. Lists 1 through 4 are still made from those working files by Steps 8 through 13. With recording off, the game plays as it does today.

15.3 Asking at the start of every game

At startup, and on new, load, and preset, the program asks:

Save this game's information to the permanent AI learning files?
Y — Yes
N — No

Y saves this game. N saves nothing and does not delete a game already saved. perft and search do not ask.

On Y, the Step 6 search of the current position finishes before the move is confirmed, so the future-search file copied for that move is the finished six-ply file. The program shows the search progress while it waits. On N, confirmation does not wait.

15.4 One archive file for the whole game

Each recorded game has one archive file, ai_learning/game_<id>.txt, inside the game-files directory. It holds both White and Black. The file is append-only. One game is not split across files. Saved games are not committed to Git. The game-files directory is already excluded.

The file begins with a GAME START block. It ends with a GAME END block when the game has a result, or a GAME STOPPED block when the player quits or starts another game. If the program is killed, the next start appends GAME STOPPED and does not change any earlier byte.

15.5 What is saved at each move

Every confirmed move appends one record before Step 7 changes the working files. The record is marked with the ply, the move number, the side that moved, and the RECORD line already used in the history file.

The record has three parts, copied unchanged from the working files:

1. Past. The four past turns for each side, as the Step 5 history files hold them, including every piece of both colors and the legal moves before and after each of those turns. Use as many turns as exist.
2. Present. The current turn, copied the same way from the history file of the side that just moved.
3. Future. The finished Step 6 future-search file for the position where the player decided. It is the six-individual-move search, with the strategies read through all six moves, and it includes both players' moves. This is the one future turn Lists 1 and 3 read.

Copying the two history files and the two future-search files, whole, satisfies these three parts. The text inside them is not rewritten into a new format.

The official List 1 for both sides, and the official List 3 for every legal candidate of the side that moved, are saved with that same move. List 3 stays tied to its candidate move. The played candidate is marked PLAYED. Searched lines are labeled searched, so a searched line is not stored as a move that was played.

15.6 What is not done

- The archive does not store a FEN instead of a history file or a future-search file.
- The archive does not rebuild a legal-move listing or a search from a FEN.
- The archive does not keep only the last window of the game. Each move is saved while the working files still hold it, so the opening and the middlegame remain.
- The archive does not change the 20 strategies, the scores, or the six-ply search.
- A four-ply or five-ply reading is not the Step 6 file.

15.7 How Lists 1 and 3 use the archive

During the game, Lists 1 through 4 are produced exactly as Steps 8 through 14 say, from the working files.

After List 1 exists, a trained network may read the archive and add a separate, labeled assessment beside it. After each List 3 candidate exists, it may add a separate, labeled assessment to that candidate only. The original rows, scores, and order remain. Memories never cause a strategy to be listed. Whether a strategy is listed is decided by the 20 definitions, the current position, and the current game's history window.

Saving these copies is not training. Training happens only when a network's weights are updated, and that belongs to the separate AI repository. If there is no archive or no trained network, Lists 1 and 3 work as Steps 8 through 14 say, and the added assessment says that AI advice is unavailable.

15.8 The memory list and the limit

The saved moves of every recorded game belong to one list called memory. The list is kept in ai_learning/memory_index.txt. Each line points to one saved move by game id and ply. A game enters the list only after it has a GAME END or GAME STOPPED block, so a game does not read its own later moves or its own result. The index can be deleted and rebuilt from the archive files. Rebuilding does not change an archive file.

Until a trained network writes an order, the list stays in recording order. The network may later sort useful memories to the front. The chess program does not decide which memories are useful, and sorting does not delete a memory.

The reader returns at most 50 memories when a caller asks for a limited set. The limit can be changed later in the AI repository. The limit never deletes a memory.

15.9 Memory banks

A memory bank is a named list of references to saved moves, not a second copy of them. Banks are kept in ai_learning/banks/. The chess program creates one bank, general.txt, containing every saved move from every game that has an end block.

A task can take a more specific bank, use it while forming List 1 and List 3 for the current game, and then return to the general bank. Specific banks, including banks for other tasks such as solved grid-board puzzles, and the choice of when to switch, belong to the separate AI repository. A chess list reads chess banks. Creating a bank does not change the four working files.

15.10 Tests

- For a recorded move, the saved history files and the saved future-search file match the four working files at that moment, byte for byte.
- Each saved history file lists every piece of both colors and the legal moves before and after the move.
- Each saved future-search file is the finished six-ply file. From the starting position that search still counts 124,132,536 nodes, and the strategies are read through all six moves.
- A game longer than the rolling window still has its first move, including the opening, at the end of the archive. The middlegame and the endgame are there as well.
- The game has one archive file, and it contains both White and Black. Each move in it has a past, a present, and a future.
- N changes no saved byte. A new game still clears the four working files and does not clear the archive. Several recorded games accumulate.
- A killed game receives GAME STOPPED on the next start, and the earlier bytes are unchanged.

- The memory index can be rebuilt to the same result. A game is absent from the index until it ends. The limit returns at most 50 memories and deletes nothing. The general bank contains the finished games.
- No test passes by rebuilding a file from a FEN.
- Every earlier test still passes.
